## 1. Cover (0:00, about 20 s)
Hi, I'm Girish. This is Down Memory Lane, a Slack bot for InspireWorks. You type a sentence like "me at five on a beach", and about ten seconds later a photo of you as a child arrives in the same thread. Every photo on this slide was made by the bot.

## 2. The brief (0:20, about 20 s)
The brief has three parts. Users ask for a photo in Slack, in their own words. The photo comes from a Flux LoRA, a small add-on trained on one person's photos so the base model can draw them. And the result comes back in the same thread. I built all three, trained on my own photos.

## 3. Bot flow (0:40, about 25 s)
Here's the flow. A user sends a message. The bot works out the age and the scene, and replies straight away so nobody stares at a blank screen. Then it calls the model on Replicate, about ten seconds, uploads the picture into the same thread, and logs the request. The two bubbles at the bottom are a real exchange. Let me show it live.

## 4. Architecture (1:50, about 40 s)
Architecture. Slack is the front door. The bot is a small Python service on Slack Bolt, using Socket Mode, so it opens an outbound connection and needs no public URL or hosting. Replicate runs the image model. A message goes one way, and the photo comes back the other. The bottom row is one-time setup: 41 captioned photos go into Replicate's Flux LoRA trainer, and the result is a private model the bot calls. Training happens once; nothing about it runs per request.

## 5. Data flow (2:30, about 30 s)
Data flow, using one real message. The Slack event carries the text, thread and user. The parser extracts an age, five, and a scene, on a beach. The prompt builder turns those into the exact wording the model was trained on, which is the key piece of logic. Guardrails then allow, decline or rate-limit, and ignore duplicate deliveries so nobody is billed twice. Replicate returns a JPEG, and the bot uploads it and logs the result.

## 6. Training (3:00, about 35 s)
Training. I trained on 41 photos of myself: 21 adult, 7 from childhood and 13 from my teens. It took about fifteen minutes, about a dollar thirty-seven: 899 seconds of GPU at Replicate's published rate. The key choice is the captions. Each one states the age, so the model learns that my face and my age are separate things. Without that, asking for me at five would give an adult face on a small body.

## 7. Prompt logic (3:35, about 35 s)
This is where the business thinking lives. Users should never need a trigger word. They type "me at five on a beach", and the bot rewrites it into the format the model was trained on. First-person words become the trained identity, the age matches my captions, and the film-photo style makes it feel like a family album scan. For child ages, the bot always names clothing. I learned that when my first test came out shirtless: the model ignores a vague "fully clothed". If no age is given, it picks seven and says so.

## 8. Results (4:10, about 20 s)
The three scenes from the brief, generated by the bot. Getting these right took three fixes. Early versions put a dot on the forehead, because baby photos in my training set had a bindi. I fixed that at the source by naming the bindi in those captions, so the model stopped treating it as part of my face. A standing two-year-old isn't realistic, so the bot now seats toddlers. And when I asked for age 17 it drew a small child, so I added photos from my teens and gave teen ages their own wording. In production, onboarding would ask each person for photos across ages.

## 9. Guardrails and privacy (4:30, about 30 s)
Guardrails matter more when the subject is a child. Child ages always get named clothing, and requests for swimwear, baths or undressed scenes are declined with a friendlier suggestion. I only caught my shirtless test image by looking at real outputs, so I'd never ship without reviewing samples. Spend is capped at eight photos per person per hour, and duplicate Slack deliveries are ignored. The model is private, and a production version would require each person's consent before training.

## 10. Cost and scale (5:00, about 25 s)
Measured over 73 photos: 7.4 seconds of GPU time per image on average, about ten seconds end to end. About a dollar thirty-seven to onboard a person and a little over a cent per image come from those measured GPU seconds at Replicate's published rate. At scale we'd add a queue and webhooks, one private model per person, and dashboards from the request log.

## 11. Roadmap (5:25, about 20 s)
From prototype to product. Next: onboarding where each person uploads ten to twenty photos, with consent first, plus an async queue. Later: family albums, delivery over WhatsApp or MMS through Plivo, and a classroom story mode. Slack is the first front door. Thank you, I'm happy to take questions.

## Likely questions from the panel
- **Why Socket Mode?** No public URL, no hosting, and it's the fastest way to a working demo. For production, switch to HTTP events behind a load balancer.
- **Why does "me" not work as the trigger word?** It's one of the most common words in English, so training on it would damage the base model. The bot rewrites "me" to the trigger instead.
- **How do you handle many users?** One private LoRA per person, created in an onboarding flow, plus a queue and webhooks so the bot never waits on Replicate.
- **What about privacy?** The model is private, and the photos aren't stored by the bot. A production version needs explicit consent per person.
- **What went wrong along the way?** The first test image was shirtless because "fully clothed" was too vague for Flux; naming the clothing fixed it. Replicate also rate-limits accounts with under $5 of credit.
